01 · The Thesis

Why this works, in three sentences

Your salary is not low because your skills are weak. It is low because of where those skills are parked — and 2026 happens to be the single best year in a decade to move them.

I looked at what the Indian market is actually paying for right now, and the gap between your current pay and your market price comes down to three independent levers. You are going to pull all three at once over the next 90 days. Each one alone is worth money; together they are what turns ₹3.4 lakh into a double-digit number.

01
The services-to-product arbitrage
The same job title pays radically differently depending on who signs the cheque. Reported 2026 benchmarks put a Java developer with five years at a services firm around ₹8–12L, and the same five years at a product company or global capability centre at ₹18–25L. At senior level the spread widens to roughly ₹18–24L versus ₹35–50L. Nothing about the engineer changes. Only the employer's business model does.
→ Lever worth 2–3× on its own
02
The AI premium, while it is still a premium
Indian salary data through mid-2026 shows professionals who can demonstrate practical, production AI competence commanding roughly a 25–40% wage premium over peers who cannot, with one Indian study putting it at 38%. Reported demand outstrips supply by something like 10 to 1. Fresher-level AI engineer ranges at product companies are quoted at ₹8–18L. This window closes as supply catches up. It has not caught up yet.
→ Lever worth a further 25–40%
03
The Java-AI gap nobody is filling
Almost everyone chasing the AI premium is doing it in Python, and almost every enterprise that needs AI in production runs on the JVM. Between May and July 2026 the Java AI stack finally settled — Spring AI reached 2.0 GA, LangChain4j shipped agent support, the Spring AI AgentCore SDK went GA. You already have deep Spring Boot muscle. You do not have to abandon your stack to enter the highest-paying niche in the market. You have to point it there.
→ Lever that makes you rare rather than one of many
The single sentence version

You are going to spend 90 days becoming "the Spring Boot engineer who has shipped a production RAG and agent service, with evaluation, cost metering and observability" — and then sell that to product companies and GCCs instead of to a services firm. That sentence is currently rare enough in India to be worth several times what you are paid.

Three things I am going to be honest about

Honesty 1 · The GCC experience wall is real

An analysis of 5,000+ live GCC openings and 20,000+ GCC hires over the past year found about 62% of hiring concentrated at three to eight years of experience, with median experience around six years. You have roughly one year. This means a pure "apply only to GCCs" strategy will mostly bounce.

So the plan targets a portfolio of four employer types — product companies and unicorns, funded AI-first startups, mid-market SaaS, and the genuinely junior GCC requisitions — weighted toward the ones that hire on demonstrated capability rather than years served. That is why so much of this plan is about building visible proof instead of collecting certificates.

Honesty 2 · ₹12–18L is the 90-day target, not ₹40L

The ₹35–50L numbers in the market reports attach to senior engineers. They are real, and they are your 2029 number, not your December number. Anyone telling you that one year of experience plus 90 days of study produces ₹40L is selling you something.

What 90 focused days can genuinely produce is a written offer in the ₹10–18L range, with a credible shot at the low ₹20s if two offers collide. That is a 3× to 5× jump. It is also the move that makes the ₹35L+ number reachable in two more jumps, because your next raise compounds off the new base — not off ₹3.4 lakh.

Honesty 3 · Location is a real decision you have to make in Week 1

The pay premiums above are concentrated in Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Pune, Gurgaon and Chennai. Ahmedabad and GIFT City are growing and are within reach of Nadiad, but the deepest market and the highest offers are not in Gujarat. Fully-remote roles at this pay band exist but are the most competitive segment of all.

You do not have to want to relocate. You do have to decide, on Day 4, which of these you will accept — because it changes which companies you apply to and it is the first question every recruiter asks. Undecided candidates lose offers to decided ones.

The refusal you got was not a verdict on your worth. It was a price quote from one buyer. This plan is about getting other quotes.

The rule that protects the whole plan

Never volunteer your current CTC before you have stated your expectation. Your line — rehearsed, on Day 59 — is to move straight to the market: "For this scope and stack I'm targeting ₹14 lakh, which is where I see comparable roles priced. I'd rather we check the fit on scope first — happy to share my current structure once we're both serious." Then stop talking.

How to actually apply — the part that decides your response rate
  • Naukri is not optional in India. It is where in-house recruiters actively source, and an incomplete profile is invisible. Complete it to 100%, refresh your résumé weekly so you resurface in searches, and set expected CTC to ₹14L.
  • Extract keywords from the job descriptions. Pull the 10–15 phrases that recur across 20 target JDs — "Spring Boot", "microservices", "Kafka", "Kubernetes", "REST APIs", "AWS" — and make sure they appear naturally in your headline, skills and bullets. Screening is keyword-first, human-second.
  • Referral beats application by a wide margin. Cold applications convert in the low single digits; a warm referral or direct recruiter contact reportedly runs 15–30% response. Every application to a Bucket A or B company should be paired with one human being contacted.
  • Apply to roles marked 2–4 years. Stated experience ranges are a filter, not a law, and strong proof routinely beats a year of tenure. Do not self-reject — that is free rejection you are performing on the company's behalf.
  • Every application gets a row in the ledger. Company, role, date, channel, referrer, status, next action. Without this you will lose track by week four and start double-applying, which recruiters notice.
07 · The Gauntlet

Every round, and what it is really testing

Interview loops for backend roles in India are predictable. Six round types, in roughly this order. Knowing what each one is actually measuring is worth more than another fifty DSA problems.

Round 1Recruiter screen15–25 min · phone

Not technical. It is a filter on three things: are your years and stack plausible, is your salary expectation inside their band, and can you hold a conversation. More candidates die here than at any coding round, almost always by fumbling the CTC question or sounding bitter about their current job.

What to have ready, verbatim
  • A 45-second summary of yourself that ends on your strongest proof, not on your job title.
  • Your expected CTC — ₹14L — said without apology and without a question mark at the end.
  • Your notice period, confirmed in writing beforehand. "I'll have to check" reads as unserious.
  • Your location answer, decided on Day 4. Not "I'm flexible", which sounds like you have not thought about it.
  • Your reason for leaving, forward-looking and free of grievance. Scripted on Day 49.
Round 2Online assessment / DSA screen60–90 min

Two or three problems, usually easy-to-medium, occasionally with MCQs on Java and SQL. This is a pure throughput filter and it is the single most mechanical thing to prepare for — which is why the plan front-loads a daily DSA habit from Day 1 rather than cramming it later.

Scope that is enough

Roughly 150 well-understood problems across the standard patterns: hashing, two pointers, sliding window, binary search, stacks, linked lists, trees and BFS/DFS, heaps, graphs, intervals, and 1-D dynamic programming. Patterns, not volume. Solving 400 problems without naming the pattern you used is worse preparation than 150 with notes.

Round 3Core Java, Spring & SQL depth45–60 min · live

Your strongest round, and the one you can make decisive. Expect collections internals, equals and hashCode, immutability, concurrency, JVM memory and garbage collection, Spring IoC and bean lifecycle, transaction propagation, AOP, and then SQL — joins, indexing, and reading a query plan.

Where candidates fold, and you will not
  • Concurrency beyond the textbook. Not just "synchronized locks the method" but a race condition you have actually caused and fixed. Day 54.
  • Transaction propagation. What REQUIRES_NEW does inside a failing outer transaction. Almost nobody can answer this cleanly. Day 42.
  • Query plans. EXPLAIN ANALYZE, index choice, and a real N+1 you found and killed. Day 34.
  • Java 21, not Java 8. Records, sealed types, pattern matching and virtual threads. Most services-firm candidates are still mentally on Java 8 and it shows within two minutes. Days 5–6.
Round 4Low-level design60 min · code or class diagram

Design a parking lot, an elevator bank, a rate limiter, a notification service, Splitwise. They are testing whether you can turn a vague requirement into clean classes with sane responsibilities — which is precisely the day job. At 1–3 years this round is often weighted more heavily than system design.

The method

Clarify requirements out loud. Name the entities. Define interfaces before implementations. Apply one or two patterns deliberately and say why — Strategy for interchangeable pricing, Factory for object creation, Observer for notifications. Then let them push on extension: "what if we add a second vehicle type?" Good design answers that without rewriting.

Round 5High-level system design45–60 min · whiteboard

URL shortener, news feed, chat, rate limiter at scale, a payment flow. At your experience level they are not expecting a staff engineer. They want to see structured thinking and honest awareness of trade-offs rather than buzzword bingo.

The six-step frame — use it every time
  1. Functional and non-functional requirements. Ask, do not assume.
  2. Back-of-envelope scale: users, QPS, storage, read-write ratio.
  3. API surface. Concrete endpoints.
  4. Data model and the storage choice, with a reason.
  5. High-level diagram: clients, gateway, services, cache, queue, database.
  6. Deep dive wherever they push, then name the bottlenecks yourself before they do.

Your unfair advantage here: you have actually built a seven-service system and debugged cross-service failures in it. Speak from that, not from a video course. Interviewers can tell the difference instantly.

Round 6Hiring manager & behavioural45 min

Decides your level and therefore your offer number. They are asking one question in several disguises: is this person going to be a problem, and will they still be here in two years?

You have unusually strong raw material — use it
  • Teaching five technologies solo, on a compressed timeline, to full batches. Most engineers at any level cannot stand in front of a room and teach Spring Boot. Frame it as evidence of depth and communication, never as "I wasn't doing real dev work".
  • Interviewing candidates on Spring Boot and Angular. You have been trusted to judge other engineers' competence. Say so.
  • Shipping AnimaFlow solo — a real SaaS with a working pipeline, payments and an end-to-end test suite. That is ownership, and ownership is what they are buying.
  • The pay negotiation itself. You built a paper trail, escalated deliberately, kept leverage in reserve and stayed factual. Told without bitterness, that is a maturity story most 22-year-olds cannot tell.

Negotiating the written offerDay 73 · after the offer letter arrives
"Thank you — I'm genuinely excited about this, and I want to say clearly that I intend to accept. One thing I'd like to work through. The fixed component is ₹[X]. Based on what I've seen for Java plus production GenAI work at this scope in [city], and given I'm bringing [the specific thing they liked in the loop], I was targeting ₹[X + 20%] fixed. If the band is capped, I'd look at it in this order: first fixed pay, then a joining bonus to bridge the gap, then an early review at six months rather than twelve. Can we get to ₹[target] fixed?" THEN STOP TALKING. Let the silence sit. Do not fill it. IF THEY IMPROVE IT: "That works. Please send the revised letter and I'll sign today." IF THEY HOLD FIRM: "Understood. Can you do [joining bonus / 6-month review / the title]?" — take the secondary item. Then accept, or decline cleanly if it's genuinely below your floor.
Mechanics that matter: negotiate only after the written offer exists, never on the verbal call. Enthusiasm first, then one number, then one reason, then silence — filling your own silence is how candidates negotiate against themselves. Ask for your highest-priority item first and concede downward in a planned order. And negotiate fixed pay: an Indian CTC headline bundles variable pay, benefits and notional ESOP value, so two identical-looking offers can differ substantially in what reaches your account. Your in-hand is typically 20–30% below the headline.

  • One page. Single column. No tables, no text boxes, no photo, no skill bar charts. Multi-column layouts get scrambled by applicant tracking systems, which is how strong candidates vanish before a human ever looks.

10 · Checkpoints & Hard Rules

Decide now what will make you change course

Pre-commit while you are thinking clearly. Four checkpoints, each with a pass condition and a specific corrective action — so that when a week goes badly you adjust a variable instead of re-litigating the whole plan.

Checkpoint 1 · Day 21 · Wed 14 October
Is the machine running? 60+ applications sent, résumé and all three profiles live, 30+ DSA problems solved, Project 1 answering questions over your own documents.
If yes: the hardest part is behind you. Momentum now does the work.
If no — and specifically if applications are the thing that slipped: that is the failure that matters. Skills can be learned late; a pipeline started late cannot be caught up, because interview loops take four to six weeks to run. Drop one study block and spend it applying, every day, until you are back on count.
Checkpoint 2 · Day 42 · Wed 4 November
Have you had at least two first-round interviews?
If yes: your positioning works. Keep volume up and start converting.
If no: the problem is your résumé or your targeting, not the market — 120+ applications with zero screens is a document problem, essentially always. Get three engineers outside TCS to tear it apart before you send another one, and check that you are not applying only to senior requisitions.
Checkpoint 3 · Day 63 · Wed 25 November
Is Project 1 deployed and public, and are you in at least one late-stage process?
If yes: you are on track for an offer inside the window. Shift weight from learning to closing.
If the project is not deployed: stop adding features today and ship what exists. An imperfect live system beats a perfect local one by an enormous margin — nobody can interview a localhost URL.
If loops are stalling at the technical round: the gap is interview performance, not knowledge. Book three paid mocks in the next ten days. It is the highest-return money in this entire plan.
Checkpoint 4 · Day 90 · Tue 22 December
Do you have a signed written offer above ₹10L?
If yes: you tripled your income in 90 days at 22. Bank the lesson: your pay was never about your ability, it was about your position and your proof. Then immediately write the next 90-day plan — that is where the following multiple comes from.
If you have offers but all below ₹10L: take the best one anyway if the stack and the team are stronger. Breaking out of the services band is worth more than the last ₹2 lakh, because your next raise compounds off the new base.
If you have no offer: you are not back where you started. You have two deployed systems, 250+ DSA problems, a real network and a live pipeline. Run a 30-day extension focused purely on conversion — mocks and closing, no new learning. Almost nobody who reaches Day 90 of this with the artefacts built stays at ₹3.4L for another year.

Nine hard rules

Break these and the plan stops working
  1. Never stop applying, not even while interviewing. The moment your pipeline empties you lose the ability to walk away, and the ability to walk away is your negotiating power. This is the most commonly broken rule and the most expensive.
  2. Never take a certification instead of shipping something. Certifications feel like progress and are the highest-risk way to lose six weeks. Your cancelled Wings T15 roadmap already cost you that once — do not rebuild it under a new name.
  3. Never reveal your current CTC before stating your expectation. Section 08 has the words. Rehearse them out loud, not in your head.
  4. Never accept an offer on the phone. Always: "thank you, I'm excited — please send the written breakup, I'll respond within 48 hours." Then negotiate. Accepting verbally forfeits the only moment the number is actually movable.
  5. Never ship a project without a README that sells it. Architecture diagram, the evaluation numbers, the cost numbers, a demo GIF, and a short "why I made these decisions" section. Unexplained code is invisible code.
  6. Never badmouth TCS in an interview. Your network there is a twenty-year asset, and interviewers hear a complaint about a past employer as a preview of you complaining about them.
  7. Never skip the back protocol. 50/10 blocks and daily extensions. You have documented disc damage at 22 and this plan adds several hundred hours of sitting. A back that fails ends every career plan you have, not just this one.
  8. Never go more than 48 hours without a commit. The public contribution graph is read as a proxy for consistency, and consistency is what you are selling to a company deciding whether one year of experience is enough.
  9. Never re-run the whole decision when a week goes badly. You have a plan with checkpoints. Between checkpoints, execute. Reopening the strategy every fortnight is itself the thing that has cost you the most time — adjust one variable at the checkpoint, then get back to work.
What success actually looks like on Day 90

A signed offer between ₹10L and ₹18L. Two deployed systems with public URLs and READMEs a stranger can follow. Roughly 250 DSA problems with pattern notes you wrote yourself. Two or three technical posts published under your name. A résumé that prices you correctly. Around 400 applications and a network of engineers at 60 companies who know who you are. And a clean exit from TCS with your references intact.

None of that depends on luck, a visa, an exam result, or anyone's permission. It depends on opening this file tomorrow morning and doing Day 1.

You spent a year fighting for a raise inside a band whose ceiling was set before you arrived. The same effort, pointed outward for 90 days, is worth several times more. Change the target, not the intensity.
