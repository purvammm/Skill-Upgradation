Today is the last full mock: four Codeforces problems in 180 minutes, started at the clock time of your reporting time on 6 December. It is called a dress rehearsal because everything you can control should be the same as on the day: the hour, the empty desk, the paper notes, the rules. The plan allows no new problems after this mock, only review, so it is also the last chance to see how your process behaves under pressure and to repair it.

Four mocks have taught you more than any single algorithm can: where your minutes go, which mistakes you repeat, and what you do when you are stuck. This chapter turns that experience into a written **playbook** for the real assessment. @sec-27-playbook says what a playbook is and what goes on it. @sec-27-minutes gives the minute-by-minute plan for three and for four problems, @sec-27-rules the decision rules for switching problems and submitting a brute force, with an argument for why brute force first costs so little, and @sec-27-checklist the checks to run before every submission. @sec-27-setup and @sec-27-notes cover the start of the mock: the setup at your reporting time and the paper notes of the first 15 minutes. @sec-27-protocol is the protocol for today and the review afterwards, and @sec-27-after gives the key idea of each mock problem, to be read only after you have tried them.

## The exam playbook {#sec-27-playbook}

During an exam you make dozens of small decisions. Should I keep debugging this? Is it time to submit the slow version? Do I start the fourth problem or polish the third? Each decision made on the spot costs minutes, and it is made by a tired person who has just spent forty minutes on the problem in front of them and does not want that time to have been wasted. The fix is to make the decisions before the exam, when you are calm, and to write them down.

:::definition{#def-27-playbook} Exam playbook
An [[exam playbook]] is a short written plan for the assessment: the time blocks, the rules that decide when to leave a problem or submit a fallback, and the checks you run before each submission. You write it before the exam, rehearse it in a mock, and do not renegotiate it during the exam.
:::

:::definition{#def-27-rule} Decision rule
A [[decision rule]] pairs a condition you can check in a few seconds with an action. "Minute 100 and the second problem is not finished: submit its brute force and move on" is a decision rule. "Don't get stuck" is not, because nothing tells you when it applies. A good rule names a time or an event you can observe, never a feeling.
:::

Most of the content already exists. The plan's exam-day page fixes the 180 minutes and five rules, the mock rules add the 15 minutes of reading and the minute-150 deadline, and the error log of Chapter 9 names the nine causes of lost marks. What this chapter adds is a version for four problems, explicit triggers for leaving a problem, a checklist, and a format.

The format is one side of one sheet, your **playbook card**. It holds four things:

1. the time table of @sec-27-minutes, in the version for three problems and the one for four;
2. six to eight decision rules (@tbl-27-rules);
3. the pre-submission checklist of @sec-27-checklist;
4. your three personal rules: the rules attached to the three causes that appear most often in your own error log.

The last item is the one that differs from reader to reader. If your log shows two overflow entries and two misread statements, "cast the first factor of every product to `long`" and "write the output format of the first sample before coding" go on the card. Workbook Exercise 27.1 has the space to write it.

:::infosys
The plan's summary of the assessment: three coding problems in three hours, worth 20, 30 and 50 marks, scored by the test cases you pass, so partial solutions score. A 2026 off-campus candidate described an in-person proctored lab round with about four DSA problems, and the official sample paper has a fourth "Complex" problem. Your playbook therefore needs a plan for three problems and a plan for four.

Each block has a reason.

* **Read everything first.** The labels Q1, Q2, Q3 are a guess by the setter about difficulty, and your strengths are not the setter's guess. Reading all problems lets you start with the one you are surest of, and a problem you have read keeps working in the back of your mind while you type another.
* **The surest problem gets 45 minutes.** It is the cheapest full marks on the paper. Finishing it calmly, with edge cases tested, is worth more than finishing it ten minutes faster with a bug.
* **Minute 100 is a hard stop for the second problem.** By then it has had 40 minutes. If it is still not finished, a submitted brute force secures part of its marks and frees the last hour.
* **The last 15 minutes write no new code.** A change made at minute 172 is tested by nobody. The block exists to check that every submission compiles and is the version you meant.

For four problems the first two hours do not change, because the plan's checkpoint advice for exam day is to lock in Q1 and Q2 first. What changes is the last hour. With two problems still open, the cheapest marks left are their brute forces, so both come first. @sec-27-rules shows why that order costs so little.

### Why brute force first costs little

The rule that feels most wrong under pressure is the second one: when you have no idea, spend ten minutes on code you know is too slow. It feels like time taken from the real solution. The following claim measures exactly what it can cost.

:::claim{#thm-27-brute} What brute force first can cost
Suppose *T* minutes remain for a problem. A brute force takes *b* ≤ *T* minutes to write and test and earns β marks. A full solution would take *f* minutes and earn *F* > β marks, and unfinished code earns nothing. Compare plan F, which works only on the full solution, with plan B, which writes and submits the brute force first and then works on the full solution. Then:

1. if *f* ≤ *T* − *b*, both plans earn *F*;
2. if *T* − *b* < *f* ≤ *T*, plan F earns *F* and plan B earns β;
3. if *f* > *T*, plan F earns 0 and plan B earns β.
:::

:::proof
Plan B finishes the full solution exactly when *b* + *f* ≤ *T*, and its final submission then earns *F*. Otherwise its best submission is the brute force, worth β. Plan F finishes exactly when *f* ≤ *T*, earning *F*, and otherwise has nothing that scores. The three cases cover every value of *f*, and in each one the earnings are as stated.
:::

So plan B loses only when *f* falls in a window of width *b* just below *T*, and there it loses *F* − β. Everywhere above *T* it gains β, and everywhere below the window it ties. You do not know *f* in advance, but you know something about it: the rule applies to a problem on which you had no idea after 15 minutes, and for such a problem *f* > *T* is the common case.

:::example{#ex-27-q3} The hard problem at minute 120
Take the three-problem paper at minute 120 with 45 minutes left for Q3, worth 50 marks. A brute force takes 12 minutes and passes about a fifth of the tests, so β = 10. @tbl-27-claim lists the marks of the two plans for several values of *f*. Plan B loses only for 33 < *f* ≤ 45.

The table also flatters plan F: it assumes that the fast solution, written without an oracle, is correct the first time. The brute force is the oracle that makes a wrong fast solution visible (Chapter 9).
:::

The same comparison explains the minute-150 rule. Q1 earns 20 marks in about 45 minutes, about 0.44 marks per minute, and Q2 earns 30 in about 60, 0.50 per minute. A 12-minute brute force that passes a fifth of a 50-mark problem earns 10 marks, about 0.83 per minute. The first minutes spent on an untouched problem are usually the best-paid minutes left in the exam, which is why no problem should reach minute 150 without a submission. The plan's rule says the same thing more briefly: a correct brute force scores more than a fast wrong solution.

:::pitfall
The share of tests a brute force passes is unknown until you try. The plan's walkthrough of the sample paper found that trying every subsequence of the Hard problem works up to *N* ≈ 20, which passes the small test files. Some problems have few small tests. Use the limits table of Chapter 9 to estimate which tests your brute force can finish, write that estimate in your notes, and do not let a hoped-for share justify skipping the rule.
:::

## Before you press submit {#sec-27-checklist}

The samples in a statement are chosen to explain the problem, not to break your code. Most lost marks in the earlier mocks came from errors that the samples cannot see: overflow on the large tests, a crash on *n* = 1, a forgotten `flush`. A short fixed list catches them.

:::definition{#def-27-checklist} Pre-submission checklist
A [[pre-submission checklist]] is a fixed list of checks that you run, in the same order, on every solution before you submit it. Each item names one error and the quickest way to see it.
:::

:::checklist Before every submission
1. **Output format.** Compare your output with the first sample character by character: spaces, line breaks, 1-based indices, the exact spelling of "YES".
2. **Samples.** Every sample of the statement gives the expected output.
3. **Edge cases.** *n* = 1, all values equal, the largest values (the plan's three). For trees, a chain.
4. **Types.** `long` for sums and products, the first factor cast before multiplying, the modulus taken after every multiplication.
5. **Input.** The reading order matches the samples, not only the format text (the plan's Lesson 2). If there is a function stub, its name, parameters and return type are unchanged.
6. **Many test cases.** Nothing carries over from one test case to the next, and nothing of the maximum size is cleared in every test case.
7. **Recursion.** The depth stays below a few thousand, or the traversal is iterative (Chapter 18).
8. **Output plumbing.** One `out.flush()` at the end, no debugging prints left, Java 8 syntax only.
9. **Version.** The file you submit calls the method you mean: the fast one, or the brute force if that is what you decided to submit.
:::

The order runs from the most frequent and cheapest check to the rarest. Items 1–3 take a minute together, because they are runs of the program. Items 4–9 are a read through the code with one question per item; each takes a few seconds once it is a habit. Item 6 needs one multiplication: the cost of one test case times the number of test cases, as in the pitfall of Chapter 9.

:::pitfall
A leftover `System.out.println("dbg " + x)` turns a correct solution into a wrong answer on the first test. Codeforces ignores standard error, so `System.err` is safe there, but you do not know what the exam platform does with it. Delete debugging lines before you submit.
:::

### One file with both versions

The checklist's item 9 and the brute-force rules both become easier if the fast method and the brute force live in the same file from the start. @lst-27-skeleton is the shape to type at the start of every problem: FastScanner from Chapter 1, one `solve` and one `brute`, and a `main` that calls one of them. Submitting the fallback is then a one-word change.

```java {#lst-27-skeleton} The exam file: one reader, a fast method and a brute force side by side
import java.io.*;
import java.util.*;

public class Main {
    public static void main(String[] args) throws IOException {
        FastScanner fs = new FastScanner(System.in);
        PrintWriter out = new PrintWriter(new BufferedWriter(new OutputStreamWriter(System.out)));
        int t = fs.nextInt();                          // one test case only? delete this loop
        while (t-- > 0) {
            int n = fs.nextInt();
            long[] a = new long[n];
            for (int i = 0; i < n; i++) a[i] = fs.nextLong();
            out.println(solve(n, a));                  // to submit the fallback, call brute(n, a)
        }
        out.flush();                                   // without this, nothing is printed
    }

    // The fast method. Example problem: the largest sum of a non-empty subarray.
    static long solve(int n, long[] a) {
        long best = Long.MIN_VALUE, cur = 0;
        for (long x : a) {
            cur = Math.max(x, cur + x);
            best = Math.max(best, cur);
        }
        return best;
    }

    // The brute force, written first: obviously correct, O(n^2), passes the small tests.
    static long brute(int n, long[] a) {
        long best = Long.MIN_VALUE;
        for (int l = 0; l < n; l++) {
            long s = 0;
            for (int r = l; r < n; r++) {
                s += a[r];
                best = Math.max(best, s);
            }
        }
        return best;
    }

    static final class FastScanner {                   // Chapter 1, unchanged
        private final BufferedReader br;
        private StringTokenizer st;

        FastScanner(InputStream in) {
            br = new BufferedReader(new InputStreamReader(in));
        }

        String next() throws IOException {
            while (st == null || !st.hasMoreTokens()) {
                String line = br.readLine();
                if (line == null) return null;
                st = new StringTokenizer(line);
            }
            return st.nextToken();
        }

        int nextInt() throws IOException { return Integer.parseInt(next()); }

        long nextLong() throws IOException { return Long.parseLong(next()); }
    }
}
```

The example problem is the maximum subarray sum of Chapter 9, so you can check the file against the stress tester you already know: on 20,000 random arrays of length up to 8 with values in −10..10, `solve` and `brute` agree. Given three test cases, [1, −2, 3], [−1, −2, −3, −4] and [2, −1, 2, −1, 2] with the last one spread over several lines, it prints 3, −1 and 4. Because `FastScanner` reads tokens and ignores line breaks, the same `main` works for input written one value per line, which is the style of the official sample paper.

When the problem gives a function stub instead of `main`, keep the stub exactly as given, put your `brute` next to it as a second method, and let the stub call whichever version you decide to submit.

## Paper notes in the first fifteen minutes {#sec-27-notes}

The plan asks you to spend the first 15 minutes reading and writing, not typing. For four problems that is about three to four minutes per problem. The notes have one row per problem, matching the workbook's planning grid.

* **Limits.** The largest *n*, the largest value, the number of test cases and any bound on the sum of *n*. These decide the target complexity before you have an idea (Chapter 16).
* **Idea.** One sentence. If you cannot write one, write the model instead: the objects, the question type and the limits.
* **Complexity.** The cost of the idea at the limits, as a number of steps.
* **Brute-force fallback.** What it enumerates, its cost, and the largest *n* it finishes. Write this line even when you think you know the full solution; it is the plan for the moment the full solution fails.
* **Edge cases.** The two or three inputs most likely to break the idea.
* **Order.** How sure you are, from 1 to 5. Start with the highest.

:::example{#ex-27-notes} One row of paper notes
Here is the row for a made-up problem: *n* lanterns stand on a line at given positions, and you must choose the smallest radius *d* such that *k* lamps placed anywhere light every lantern. The notes take three minutes to write.

```trace
Q?  lanterns
limits  t <= 1e4, sum n <= 2e5, positions <= 1e9, k <= n
idea    binary search on d; check(d): greedy, place each lamp at the
        leftmost unlit lantern + d, count lamps <= k
cost    O(n log n) sort + O(n log 1e9) checks, about 6e6 steps
brute   try d = 0, 1, 2, ... with the same check: only for positions <= 1000
edges   n = 1 (d = 0); all positions equal; positions 1e9 (long for p + 2d)
sure    4 of 5   ->  order 1
```

The brute force here shares the check with the fast version, so it tests the search and its bounds, not the greedy. That is worth knowing in advance: if the fast version and the brute force agree but the answer is wrong, the bug is in the check (Chapter 9).
:::

Two habits make the notes pay. Read the output section before the story, because it tells you what the question type is. And when two problems seem equally sure, start with the one with the smaller weight: it is the one most likely to be finished quickly, and a finished problem early in the exam is good for the next two hours.

### The review

The afternoon is for review only; the plan says no new problems after this mock. The review follows the steps of the earlier mocks, about 60 minutes in all.

1. **Score.** Enter each problem's estimated share of tests and the weighted score.
2. **Read and rewrite.** For each problem you did not finish, read the editorial, close it, and write the solution yourself. This is review, not a new problem: you have already spent the thinking time on it.
3. **Log the cause** of every failure with the nine causes of Chapter 9: misread statement, wrong idea, overflow or mod, off-by-one, TLE or complexity, input reading, edge case, stack overflow, ran out of time.
4. **Check the process, not only the code.** Write down, from your notes, the minute at which each problem started and ended. Did you start the surest problem at minute 15? Did minute 100 and minute 150 trigger what the rules say? A process failure ("I kept debugging Q2 until minute 130") is logged as "ran out of time" with the rule you broke.
5. **Update the playbook card.** Every cause logged today either confirms a rule already on the card or adds one. Keep the card to one side of one sheet; if it overflows, merge rules rather than shrink the writing.

:::keyidea
After this mock the most valuable thing you can still change is not your algorithms but your process. A rule written today and followed on 6 December can be worth more marks than a technique learned today.
:::

The plan's note for the day ends: this is the last full rehearsal, and after today you protect your confidence and your sleep. A bad score today is information for the playbook, not a forecast. Chapter 28 is consolidation, with no new topics; Chapter 29 is a rest day.

## After the mock {#sec-27-after}

:::pitfall Read only after the mock
This section gives the key idea of each Mock 5 problem. Reading it before the mock wastes the last rehearsal. Take the mock, grade it, write your own post-mortem, and only then compare.
:::

Each idea below was checked with my own solution, which passes the problem's samples and agrees with a brute force on random small inputs: 20,000 strings for CF 1385D, plus 3,000 more against a checker that tests the definition of a good string directly, and 5,000 random cases for each of the other three.

**CF 1385D (a-Good String).** The definition is recursive, and so is the answer. A block that must become *c*-good either makes its left half all *c*, which costs the number of letters there that are not *c*, and makes its right half (*c* + 1)-good, or does the same with the halves swapped; take the cheaper option. The recursion has log *n* levels and each level looks at every letter once, so the cost is O(*n* log *n*) per test case, and its depth is at most 18.

**CF 1446B (Catching Cheaters).** Count what each character contributes to 4 · LCS − |*C*| − |*D*|: a character matched in the common subsequence adds 4 − 2 = 2 for the pair, and every other character costs 1. So the score is a local alignment in which a match gains 2 and every skipped character loses 1, and the best substrings never start or end with a loss. A two-string DP (Chapter 12) over pairs of end positions, with 0 allowed for starting fresh, gives the answer as its largest cell in O(*nm*) = 2.5 · 10⁷ steps; two rows of the table are enough.

**CF 1324F (Maximum White Subtree).** Give each vertex +1 if it is white and −1 if it is black. Rooted at vertex 1, the best connected set inside the subtree of *v* that contains *v* is *v* plus every child's best set that has a positive value. The answer for every vertex then needs the part of the tree above it, which is the rerooting of Chapter 19: the parent's full answer, minus what *v* contributed to it, counts only if it is positive. Both passes run over a BFS order in O(*n*), with no recursion, because a chain of 2 · 10⁵ vertices would overflow the stack.

**CF 1433F (Zero Remainder Sum).** Handle the rows one at a time. For one row, a knapsack (Chapter 11) whose state is (elements taken so far, at most ⌊*m*/2⌋; sum modulo *k*) gives the largest sum the row can contribute for each remainder. A second DP over the remainder of the running total combines the rows, with unreachable remainders kept as −∞, and the answer is the entry for remainder 0, which is at least 0 because choosing nothing is allowed. The row knapsacks cost about *n* · *m* · (*m*/2) · *k* ≈ 1.2 · 10⁷ steps at the limits.

## Summary

- An exam playbook fixes before the exam the time blocks, the decision rules and the checks, so that no decision is made by a tired person in the middle of a problem. Keep it on one side of one sheet, with your three personal rules from the error log.
- The 180 minutes: read and write notes until 15, the surest problem until 60, the second until 120 (brute force at 100 if stuck), the hard problem until 165, then checks only. With four problems, the last hour starts with brute forces for both open problems.
- A decision rule names a time or an observable event and an action. "Minute 150: every problem has a submission" is a rule; "don't get stuck" is not.
- Brute force first loses only when the full solution would have fitted in the remaining time but not after the brute force, a window as wide as the brute force takes to write; above that window it gains the brute force's marks.
- Run the pre-submission checklist on every solution: format, samples, edge cases, types, input order, test-case resets, recursion depth, flush and debug prints, and the version you submit.
- Keep `solve` and `brute` in one file, so that submitting the fallback is a one-word change.
- Mock 5 starts at your real reporting time, with exam conditions and paper notes for the first 15 minutes. The checkpoint is a weighted score of 65; below it, lock in Q1 and Q2 on exam day and take partial marks on Q3.

:::answers
1. The labels are the setter's estimate of difficulty, not yours, and the surest problem is the cheapest set of full marks on the paper. Starting with Q1 because it is first risks spending the best hour on a problem that suits you less, while an easier one waits until you are tired.
2. "Minute 100 and the second problem is not finished: submit its brute force (if the current version is not better) and move to the next problem." It names a time and an action, so you can check it in a second.
3. Plan B loses when *T* − *b* < *f* ≤ *T*, that is for 20 < *f* ≤ 30. There plan F earns 30 and plan B earns 6, so plan B loses 24 marks. For *f* ≤ 20 both earn 30; for *f* > 30 plan B earns 6 and plan F nothing.
4. Each brute force is the cheapest set of marks left on its problem: a few minutes for a share of the tests, often more marks per minute than anything else in the last hour. Writing both first guarantees a submission on every problem by minute 150; optimising one first risks leaving the other blank.
5. Types (item 4): the samples are small, so a sum that overflows `int` only at the limits passes them. Edge cases (item 3): the samples rarely include *n* = 1 or all-equal values. Also many test cases (item 6): the samples have few test cases, so a per-test reset of a large array is never slow on them.
6. Submitting the fallback becomes a one-word change in `main`, made in seconds at minute 100 or 150 without copying code between files. The brute force is also at hand as the oracle for a stress test.
7. Concentration, meals, sleep and the warm-up all depend on the clock time. Rehearsing at the real hour tests that whole routine, not only the problem solving.
8. 20 · 1 + 30 · 0.8 + 50 · 0.2 = 20 + 24 + 10 = 54, with Q4's 10 % noted beside it. That is below the checkpoint of 65, so on exam day lock in Q1 and Q2 first and use partial marks on Q3.
9. "Ran out of time": the process broke, because the minute-100 rule did not trigger. The card should say it more forcefully, for example "minute 100: Q2 brute force submitted, no exceptions", and the time should be written in the notes when each problem starts, so that the trigger is visible.
