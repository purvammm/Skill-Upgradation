The question this chapter answers is how to turn the minutes you have into as many passed tests as possible when full solutions run out.

## Four problems in 180 minutes {#sec-23-four}

The plan's summary of the test reports three problems in three hours, but two of its sources point to four. A 2026 off-campus candidate described an in-person proctored round with about four DSA problems, and the official sample paper has a fourth "Complex" problem after Easy, Medium and Hard. The plan's target section quotes the clearest report it found: a candidate who solved three problems fully and passed 6 of 12 tests on a fourth was shortlisted for the SP L3 interview. A fourth problem changes the arithmetic. After 15 minutes of reading and 15 of final checks, 150 minutes remain for work, an average of 37 minutes per problem. The plan's own estimates for today's problems are 45, 45, 75 and 75 minutes, 240 in total. So at least one problem will end the mock unfinished, and the decision is which one and how much of it to keep. The day's note says it plainly: aim for full marks on two or three problems and a brute force on the rest.

## Expected marks per minute {#sec-23-ratio}

The reading phase gives you more than ideas. For each piece of work you can estimate three numbers: how many minutes it needs, how many marks it brings if it works (the problem's weight times the share of tests it passes), and the chance that it works.

:::definition{#def-23-expected} Expected marks and marks per minute
The [[expected marks]] of a piece of work is *w* = (weight of the problem) × (share of tests it passes) × (chance that it works). If it needs *p* minutes, its [[marks per minute]] is *w*/*p*.
:::

A brute force that passes the small tests is one piece; the rest of the same problem, earned by the full solution, is another piece done after it. Once the work is in pieces, the question is the order. The first fact is about one pair of pieces.

:::lemma{#lem-23-dominance} Short and valuable first is never behind
Let *X* and *Y* be two pieces of work done one after the other, with *p*<sub>*X*</sub> ≤ *p*<sub>*Y*</sub> and *w*<sub>*X*</sub> ≥ *w*<sub>*Y*</sub>. At every moment, the expected marks already secured by the order "*X*, then *Y*" are at least those of "*Y*, then *X*", whatever comes before and after the pair.
:::

:::proof
Let both orders start the pair at time *t*. Every other piece finishes at the same moment in both orders, so only the pair matters. Before *t* + *p*<sub>*X*</sub>, neither order has finished either piece of the pair. From *t* + *p*<sub>*X*</sub> until *t* + *p*<sub>*Y*</sub>, the first order has secured *w*<sub>*X*</sub> and the second nothing. From *t* + *p*<sub>*Y*</sub> until *t* + *p*<sub>*X*</sub> + *p*<sub>*Y*</sub>, the first has *w*<sub>*X*</sub> and the second *w*<sub>*Y*</sub> ≤ *w*<sub>*X*</sub>. After that both have *w*<sub>*X*</sub> + *w*<sub>*Y*</sub>.
:::

Most pairs are not that clear-cut: a short brute force is usually worth fewer marks than a long full solution. Then no order is ahead at every moment, but one is ahead on average.

:::theorem{#thm-23-ratio} The ratio rule
Pieces of work 1..*m* with times *p*<sub>*j*</sub> > 0 and expected marks *w*<sub>*j*</sub> ≥ 0 are done back to back from time 0, and *C*<sub>*j*</sub> is the moment piece *j* finishes. The sum Σ *w*<sub>*j*</sub>*C*<sub>*j*</sub> is smallest exactly for the orders that sort the pieces by *w*<sub>*j*</sub>/*p*<sub>*j*</sub>, largest first. Equivalently, these orders maximise the expected marks secured at a stopping moment chosen uniformly at random in [0, *H*], for any *H* ≥ Σ *p*<sub>*j*</sub>.
:::

:::proof
Swap two neighbours *X* (first) and *Y* that start at time *t*. All other pieces keep their finishing times. Before the swap the pair contributes *w*<sub>*X*</sub>(*t* + *p*<sub>*X*</sub>) + *w*<sub>*Y*</sub>(*t* + *p*<sub>*X*</sub> + *p*<sub>*Y*</sub>), after it *w*<sub>*Y*</sub>(*t* + *p*<sub>*Y*</sub>) + *w*<sub>*X*</sub>(*t* + *p*<sub>*X*</sub> + *p*<sub>*Y*</sub>). The difference, after minus before, is *w*<sub>*X*</sub>*p*<sub>*Y*</sub> − *w*<sub>*Y*</sub>*p*<sub>*X*</sub>, which is negative exactly when *w*<sub>*Y*</sub>/*p*<sub>*Y*</sub> > *w*<sub>*X*</sub>/*p*<sub>*X*</sub>. So an order with a neighbour pair in the wrong order can be strictly improved, and swapping two neighbours with equal ratios changes nothing. There are finitely many orders, so some order is optimal; it cannot have a wrong-order pair, so it is sorted. All sorted orders turn into one another by swaps of equal-ratio neighbours, so they all have the same, optimal, sum.

For the second statement, let *V*(*s*) be the expected marks secured by moment *s*, the sum of *w*<sub>*j*</sub> over the pieces with *C*<sub>*j*</sub> ≤ *s*. Piece *j* contributes *w*<sub>*j*</sub> to *V*(*s*) for every *s* in [*C*<sub>*j*</sub>, *H*], so the area under *V* from 0 to *H* is Σ *w*<sub>*j*</sub>(*H* − *C*<sub>*j*</sub>) = *H* · Σ *w*<sub>*j*</sub> − Σ *w*<sub>*j*</sub>*C*<sub>*j*</sub>. The expected value of *V* at a uniform random moment is that area divided by *H*, which is largest when Σ *w*<sub>*j*</sub>*C*<sub>*j*</sub> is smallest.
:::

In scheduling this is [[Smith's rule]]. The random stopping moment stands for whatever ends your progress early: a bug that eats forty minutes, an idea that turns out wrong, fatigue in the third hour.

Two cautions. The rule is only as good as your estimates, but its main decisions survive rough numbers: a brute force is short and nearly certain, so its ratio is high under almost any estimate, and the long shot at the end of a hard problem is low under almost any estimate. And the theorem assumes every piece can be finished. When they do not all fit before minute 165, choosing which to finish is a 0/1 knapsack with time as the capacity (Chapter 11), and the ratio order is a good order but not always the best.

### When to stop

:::definition{#def-23-timebox} Time box
A [[time box]] is a limit, set before you start a piece of work, on the minutes you will spend on it before you stop and re-plan.
:::

The plan sets two: minute 100 for the second problem of a three-problem paper (minute 90 in @tbl-23-minutes), and 15 minutes without a full idea on the hard problem before you write its brute force. A time box says when to re-plan; the ratio says what to do next. When a box runs out, estimate the *remaining* piece again, with the minutes it still needs and its chance now that you know more, and switch if its ratio is below that of the best piece not yet started. Stop early, before the box runs out, when two submissions have failed and you have no new hypothesis about why, or when the idea rests on a lemma you can neither prove nor stress-test.

Minutes already spent never enter the ratio. "I have put 40 minutes into this, so I must finish it" is the reasoning the rule replaces. Before you leave a problem, write in the planning grid what you would try next, so that you can resume quickly if time remains.

## Partial solutions from the limits {#sec-23-tiers}

Chapter 9 collected marks from small *n*. Small *n* is not the only restriction a test file can have.

:::definition{#def-23-partial} Partial solution
A [[partial solution]] is a program that is correct and fast enough on every input that satisfies an extra restriction (smaller limits, or a special structure such as "the tree is a path"), and may be wrong or too slow on the others. The partial solutions of one problem form a ladder of **tiers**, from the brute force up to the full solution.
:::

Some statements list subtasks with their marks; most do not, and then the limits and the objects are your only guide. These special cases most often give a short extra tier.

Table: {#tbl-23-special} Special cases that often give a tier of their own
| Object | Restrictions worth checking |
|---|---|
| array | *n* ≤ 20 (all subsets) or *n* ≤ 3000 (all pairs); all values equal; already sorted; values ≤ 10⁶, so they can index an array; all values positive |
| tree | a path (each vertex hangs below the previous one); a star; small depth; every vertex has at most two children |
| graph | a tree; no cycles; all edge weights equal, so BFS replaces Dijkstra |
| queries | a single query; no updates; all queries known in advance, so they can be answered offline |
| numbers | *K* = 1 or *K* = *n*; a small modulus; an answer that fits in `long` without the modulus |

"The tree is a path" turns many tree problems into array problems, often in ten lines. Whether the hidden tests contain paths is not published, so such a tier is a bet with a modest chance; let its marks per minute decide when to write it.

### Several tiers in one submission

Tiers have to live in one program: it reads the input, decides which tier applies, and calls it.

:::keyidea
Route every input to the most trusted tier that can handle it in time. Small inputs go to the brute force even when you have a fast solution, because the brute force is correct by construction: the small tests are then safe even if the fast tier has a bug.
:::

```java {#lst-23-tiers} Two tiers in one submission: pairs with a[i] + a[j] at most x
// Pairs i < j with a[i] + a[j] <= x. Two tiers in one submission: inputs up to SMALL go to the
// brute force, which is correct by construction, so their tests stay safe even if fast() is wrong.
static final int SMALL = 3000;                     // about 4.5e6 pair checks at the threshold

static long solve(int[] a, long x) {
    return a.length <= SMALL ? brute(a, x) : fast(a, x);
}

static long brute(int[] a, long x) {               // tier 1: every pair, O(n^2)
    long c = 0;
    for (int i = 0; i < a.length; i++)
        for (int j = i + 1; j < a.length; j++)
            if ((long) a[i] + a[j] <= x) c++;
    return c;
}

static long fast(int[] a, long x) {                // tier 2: sort, then two pointers (Chapter 3)
    int[] s = a.clone();
    Arrays.sort(s);
    long c = 0;
    for (int l = 0, r = s.length - 1; l < r; ) {
        if ((long) s[l] + s[r] <= x) { c += r - l; l++; }   // s[l] fits with all of s[l+1..r]
        else r--;                                           // s[r] fits with nobody left
    }
    return c;
}
```

:::claim{#thm-23-pointers} The fast tier counts every pair once
On the sorted copy *s*, `fast` returns the number of pairs *i* < *j* with *s*[*i*] + *s*[*j*] ≤ *x*.
:::

:::proof
The loop keeps the invariant that every pair with an index outside [*l*, *r*] has already been counted if it qualifies, and no pair inside has been counted. If *s*[*l*] + *s*[*r*] ≤ *x*, then *s*[*l*] + *s*[*m*] ≤ *x* for every *m* in (*l*, *r*], because *s*[*m*] ≤ *s*[*r*]; these are all the qualifying pairs that use *l* and lie inside, so the loop adds *r* − *l* and drops *l*. Otherwise *s*[*m*] + *s*[*r*] ≥ *s*[*l*] + *s*[*r*] > *x* for every *m* in [*l*, *r*), so no pair inside uses *r*, and the loop drops it. When *l* ≥ *r* no pair is left inside. Sorting does not change the number of qualifying pairs.
:::

The threshold comes from the cost at the threshold: *n* = 3000 means about 4.5 · 10⁶ pair checks, far below a second. The fast tier was compared with the brute force on 20,000 random arrays of up to 11 values in −10..10, and on 20 arrays of 2,900 to 3,199 values up to 10⁹ in absolute value; they agreed every time. The cast `(long) a[i] + a[j]` matters on the second set: two values near 10⁹ overflow `int`.

:::tip
Write the dispatch line first, with the brute force behind it, and submit. Then write the fast tier. If the fast tier is still wrong at minute 165, the submission on the judge still holds every small test.
:::

## Debugging under time pressure {#sec-23-debug}

Minutes spent hunting a bug are minutes not spent on the next piece of work. Try the cheap checks first, in a fixed order, and give the whole search a time box of about ten minutes.

:::checklist A ten-minute debugging order
1. Run every sample again and compare the output character by character: a misread statement or output format shows up here.
2. Print the parsed input back, to standard error, and compare it with the input file.
3. Run *n* = 1, all values equal, and the largest values (edge cases and overflow, Chapter 16).
4. Turn on the checks of the invariants (below).
5. If a brute force exists, run the stress tester of Chapter 9 and shrink the first failing input.
6. On the small failing input, print the intermediate arrays and compare them with values worked out by hand or by the brute force. The first wrong value points to the line that produced it.
:::

Step 6 searches the computation rather than the input: a DP that is wrong in its last cell went wrong in some earlier row, and printing the rows for a five-element input finds the first bad one.

### Checks that stay in the code

:::definition{#def-23-check} Runtime check
A [[runtime check]] is a test of a condition that must hold if the program and its input are as you believe, such as "the BFS reached all *n* vertices". If the condition fails, the program stops with an exception.
:::

On a judge, a failed check turns a silent wrong answer into a runtime-error verdict, which tells you that an assumption broke. Tree input is the usual suspect: 0-based or 1-based vertices, *n* − 1 edges or a parent array.

```java {#lst-23-check} A check helper, and a BFS order that checks that the input is a tree
// A check that stays in the submitted code: a failed check becomes a runtime-error verdict.
static void check(boolean ok, String what) {
    if (!ok) throw new IllegalStateException(what);
}

// BFS order of a tree with n vertices 1..n read as n - 1 edges; checks that the input is a tree.
static int[] bfsOrder(int n, int[][] edges) {
    check(edges.length == n - 1, "expected n - 1 edges, got " + edges.length);
    List<List<Integer>> g = new ArrayList<>();
    for (int v = 0; v <= n; v++) g.add(new ArrayList<Integer>());
    for (int[] e : edges) {
        check(1 <= e[0] && e[0] <= n && 1 <= e[1] && e[1] <= n,
              "vertex out of range: 0-based input?");
        g.get(e[0]).add(e[1]);
        g.get(e[1]).add(e[0]);
    }
    int[] order = new int[n];
    boolean[] seen = new boolean[n + 1];
    int head = 0, tail = 0;
    order[tail++] = 1;
    seen[1] = true;
    while (head < tail) {
        int u = order[head++];
        for (int v : g.get(u)) if (!seen[v]) { seen[v] = true; order[tail++] = v; }
    }
    check(tail == n, "only " + tail + " of " + n + " vertices reached: edges misread?");
    return order;
}
```

Given four vertices with the edges written 0-based, the method stops with "vertex out of range: 0-based input?". Given the edges 1–2, 2–1 and 3–4, which is not a tree, it stops with "only 2 of 4 vertices reached: edges misread?". A check costs a comparison or two, so it can stay in the final submission.

:::java
Java's `assert` statement is skipped unless the JVM is started with the `-ea` flag, which you normally cannot set on a judge, so @lst-23-check throws an exception itself. Print debug output with `System.err.println`: Codeforces compares only standard output. Debug lines still cost time, so guard them with `static final boolean DEBUG = false;` or delete them before the final submission.
:::

### Shrinking a failing tree

The shrinker of Chapter 9 deletes array elements. A failing tree needs its own: delete a leaf, renumber, and keep the change while the programs still disagree. Trees are parent arrays with par[0] = −1 and 0 ≤ par[*v*] < *v*, which the generator below produces.

```java {#lst-23-shrink} Random trees as parent arrays, and a shrinker that deletes leaves
// Trees as parent arrays: par[0] = -1 and 0 <= par[v] < v. A random tree, and a shrinker that
// deletes leaves while the two answers still differ.
static int[] randomTree(Random rnd, int n) {
    int[] par = new int[n];
    par[0] = -1;
    for (int v = 1; v < n; v++) par[v] = rnd.nextInt(v);
    return par;
}

static int[] deleteLeaf(int[] par, int leaf) {          // renumber the vertices after the leaf
    int[] q = new int[par.length - 1];
    for (int v = 0, k = 0; v < par.length; v++) {
        if (v == leaf) continue;
        q[k++] = par[v] > leaf ? par[v] - 1 : par[v];
    }
    return q;
}

static int[] shrinkTree(int[] par, ToLongFunction<int[]> fast, ToLongFunction<int[]> slow) {
    boolean changed = true;
    while (changed) {
        changed = false;
        boolean[] hasChild = new boolean[par.length];
        for (int v = 1; v < par.length; v++) hasChild[par[v]] = true;
        for (int v = par.length - 1; v >= 1 && !changed; v--) {
            if (hasChild[v]) continue;                    // only leaves are deleted
            int[] q = deleteLeaf(par, v);
            if (fast.applyAsLong(q) != slow.applyAsLong(q)) { par = q; changed = true; }
        }
    }
    return par;
}
```

:::lemma{#lem-23-shrink} The tree shrinker stops with a tree that still fails
If `fast` and `slow` disagree on the parent array `par`, @lst-23-shrink returns a valid parent array on which they still disagree, and deleting any single leaf of it makes them agree.
:::

:::proof
A leaf *v* ≥ 1 has no children, so no parent entry points to it, and after its removal every vertex *u* > *v* becomes *u* − 1 together with its parent if that parent is also above *v*; so par[*u*] < *u* still holds and vertex 0 stays the root. Each accepted change removes one vertex, so there are fewer than *n* of them and the loop ends. A change is accepted only while the programs disagree, so the result disagrees. The loop ends only after a full pass over the leaves in which no deletion kept the disagreement.
:::

:::example{#ex-23-shrink} The longest path does not have to pass through the root
A buggy diameter computes the longest path through the root only: the two deepest branches below vertex 0. Stress-tested against a BFS from every vertex on random trees of up to 12 vertices, it fails at once, and the shrinker reduces the first failing tree:

```trace
Mismatch on iteration 99: par=[-1, 0, 1, 1, 3, 3, 4, 2, 2, 3] fast=4 brute=5
shrunk to par=[-1, 0, 1, 1, 3, 2] fast=3 brute=4
```

On the six-vertex tree of @fig-23-shrunk the longest path is 5–2–1–3–4, four edges, and it avoids the root. The ten-vertex tree hid that; the small one shows it. On 2,000 random failing trees the shrinker's result always still failed and no single leaf deletion kept the failure, as @lem-23-shrink says.
:::

## After the mock {#sec-23-after}

:::pitfall Read only after the mock
This section gives the key idea of each Mock 3 problem. Reading it before the mock wastes the measurement. Take the mock, grade it, write your own post-mortem, and only then compare.
:::

Each idea below was checked with my own solution, which passes the problem's samples and agrees with a brute force on 20,000 random small inputs per problem: a search over all cut positions for Q1, a full game search over deletions for Q2, a search over all painting orders for Q3, and a breadth-first search over all reachable arrays for Q4.

**CF 1426D (Non-zero Segments).** A segment has sum 0 exactly when two prefix sums are equal (Chapter 2), and an inserted huge number acts as a wall that no zero-sum segment can cross. Scan left to right with a hash set of the prefix sums of the current piece, starting from {0}; when the new prefix is already in the set, put a wall just before the current element, count it, and restart the set from that element. Placing each wall as late as possible is optimal, as in interval stabbing (Chapter 4): the zero-sum segment that ends first needs a wall inside it, and the latest position breaks every segment an earlier one would. Use `long` sums.

**CF 1689C (Infected Tree).** With one deletion per step you can stop the infection from branching but not from moving: each step you delete one child of the vertex infected last, and the infection moves into the other child. Let dp[*v*] be the most vertices saved in the subtree of *v* once *v* is infected: 0 for a leaf, size(*c*) − 1 for a single child *c*, and the better of size(*c*₁) − 1 + dp[*c*₂] and size(*c*₂) − 1 + dp[*c*₁] for two children. Compute sizes and dp in reverse BFS order (Chapter 18), O(*n*) per test.

**CF 1187E (Tree Painting).** Once the first vertex *r* is fixed, the total does not depend on the order: a vertex can only be painted after its parent in the tree rooted at *r*, and at that moment its white component is exactly its subtree. So the total is the sum of all subtree sizes with root *r*. Rerooting (Chapter 19) moves the root from *p* to a child *c* in O(1), because only those two subtrees change: S(*c*) = S(*p*) + *n* − 2 · size(*c*). The answer is the largest S, in `long`.

**CF 1913D (Array Collapse).** A subsequence is reachable exactly when every element of each block of deleted elements is larger than the smaller of the block's two kept neighbours; a block before the first kept element must be larger than that element, and likewise after the last. Count the reachable subsequences by their last kept index *i*. The possible previous kept indices are those strictly between the previous smaller element of *i* and *i*, plus those on the increasing stack of Chapter 7 that are smaller than *p*[*i*], plus "none" when nothing before *i* is smaller. Prefix sums of the counts and a running sum along the stack give each count in O(1) amortised; the answer is the sum along the final stack, modulo 998244353.

## Summary

- Four problems leave about 37 minutes each after reading and checking, so plan which pieces of work to finish, not which problems.
- Estimate each piece's minutes, marks and chance. Ordering by expected marks per minute minimises Σ *w*·*C*, which maximises the marks secured at a random stopping moment; a piece that is both shorter and worth more always goes first.
- Brute forces have high ratios because they are short and nearly certain, so both remaining brute forces come before either long shot.
- Time boxes say when to re-plan; the ratio of the *remaining* work says what to do next. Minutes already spent do not count.
- Partial solutions come from small limits and from special structures. Put the tiers in one submission and send small inputs to the brute force.
- Debug cheapest check first, with a ten-minute box. Runtime checks stay in the code, `assert` is off on judges, debug output goes to standard error, and a leaf-deleting shrinker reduces a failing tree.
- Mock 3's checkpoint is a weighted score of at least 60 with a brute force on every problem. Under Plan B, 29 November is the exam itself.
